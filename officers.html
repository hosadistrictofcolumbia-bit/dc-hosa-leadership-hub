Domain 1 · BUILD Pillar

Boss vs. Leader

The title makes you an officer. The choices you make every week decide whether you're a boss or a leader. This distinction is fundamental to every leadership model in healthcare management.

A Boss Says / Does A Leader Says / Does
Controls through authority — "Do this because I said so."Earns buy-in — explains the why before asking for the what.
Takes credit when things go well.Attributes success to the team publicly and specifically.
Assigns blame when things go wrong.Owns team failures, then problem-solves together.
Gives vague feedback — "Do better."Gives specific behavioral feedback — "When you did X, the effect was Y."
Sees people as resources to deploy.Sees people as individuals with growing capabilities.
Creates dependency — members only act when told.Builds independence — members know what good looks like and self-direct.
Manages compliance.Builds commitment — members choose to show up fully.
Reacts to problems.Anticipates and prevents problems through consistent communication.
📚 Research basis

Greenleaf's servant leadership model (1970) and Bass & Avolio's transformational leadership research (1994) both confirm that leaders who prioritize developing others — rather than managing compliance — produce measurably higher team performance, retention, and cohesion. In healthcare settings specifically, transformational leadership is correlated with fewer adverse events and higher patient satisfaction scores. The Boss vs. Leader distinction is not just management theory — it predicts clinical team outcomes.

💡
Officer self-check, weekly: "Did I do or say something this week that was boss behavior? What would the leader version of that look like?" The gap between how we think we show up and how we actually show up is almost always smaller than we'd like.
Domain 2 · EMPOWER Pillar

Build Psychological Safety

Amy Edmondson's landmark research (Harvard, 1999) showed that the highest-performing teams — in hospitals, in tech companies, in military units — all share one thing: members feel safe to speak up, make mistakes, and disagree without fear of punishment. Your job as an officer is to create that environment deliberately.

🗣️ Model Vulnerability First

Edmondson's research shows psychological safety comes from the leader demonstrating it first. Share a mistake you made and what it taught you before asking members to do the same. Vulnerability at the top gives everyone else permission.

📚 Research basis

Teams in Edmondson's studies where leaders explicitly modeled "this is safe to get wrong here" reported 40% higher learning behaviors and significantly fewer critical errors (Edmondson, 1999, AMJ).

✋ Reframe Errors as Learning

When a member makes a mistake — publicly if possible — respond with "What did we learn here?" not "Why did that happen?" The first frame activates curiosity and growth; the second activates defensiveness and shutdown.

📚 Research basis

High-performing ICU teams in Edmondson's hospital studies reported more errors, not fewer — because they felt safe to surface them. Lower-performing teams hid errors, leading to worse outcomes. Surface rate is an indicator of safety, not incompetence.

🤝 Invite Disagreement Explicitly

At every meeting: "Does anyone see this differently?" "What am I missing?" "Who disagrees and why?" If no one ever challenges your ideas, it's not because your ideas are always right — it's because they don't feel safe to say so.

📚 Research basis

Google's Project Aristotle (Rozovsky, 2015) studied 180 teams and found psychological safety was the #1 predictor of team effectiveness — above intelligence, experience, or seniority of team members. The most important conversations are the ones that feel risky to have.

🏥
Healthcare parallel (Edmondson, 1999): Nurses who felt psychologically safe enough to speak up were significantly more likely to catch medication errors before they reached patients. Psychological safety in your chapter is preparation for the clinical environments where speaking up is literally life-saving. Practice it now.
Domain 3 · EMPOWER Pillar

Motivate the Team

Motivation isn't a pep talk. It's understanding what actually drives people — and building conditions where those drivers are present. Deci & Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (SDT) gives you a reliable framework.

🧭

Autonomy

People need to feel they have genuine choice in what they do — that they are self-directed, not merely compliant.

→
Delegate actual decision-making, not just tasks
→
Ask members what they want to lead — don't assume
→
Explain the why of each assignment before the what
📚 Research basis

Deci & Ryan (1985) demonstrated that intrinsically motivated individuals perform significantly better on complex tasks than those working under external pressure or reward.

Officer application →
💪

Competence

People need to feel increasingly capable — to grow, be challenged at the right level, and have their progress acknowledged.

→
Match challenge to skill — too easy = boredom, too hard = shutdown
→
Acknowledge specific growth publicly
→
Offer stretch assignments with appropriate support
📚 Research basis

Csikszentmihalyi's flow research (1990) shows that optimal performance and deepest engagement occur when task difficulty is calibrated just above current skill level.

Officer application →
🤝

Relatedness

People need to feel genuinely connected — that they belong to something and matter to the people they work with.

→
Spend 5 minutes asking how members are doing before every meeting
→
Recognize birthdays, milestones, chapter contributions
→
Make visible the connections between members' roles and the mission
📚 Research basis

Baumeister & Leary (1995) established belonging as a fundamental human need — not a preference. Chronic disconnection predicts disengagement across every organizational context studied.

Domain 4 · ADVANCE Pillar

Give Feedback That Works

Most feedback fails because it's vague, personal, or untimely. These two evidence-based frameworks give your feedback specificity and structure — and make it actually usable by the person receiving it.

🎯 SBI — Situation-Behavior-Impact

S
Situation
Anchor in a specific moment — not "sometimes" or "always"
B
Behavior
Observable action only — not character, not motive, not interpretation
I
Impact
What it meant for the team — in your own experience
📚 Research basis

Behavioral specificity reduces defensiveness because observable behavior is harder to dispute than character judgments (Losada & Heaphy, 2004). Feedback framed as observation — not interpretation — activates learning rather than self-protection.

🌱 Growth Feedback Protocol

1
Ask permission — "Can I share an observation?" Consent makes feedback 60% more likely to be heard (Scott, 2017)
2
Describe behavior, not person — "When you [specific action]" not "You always..."
3
State the impact on the team or mission — make the stakes visible
4
Ask what support looks like — "What would help you with this?"
5
Close with specific next step — a mutual commitment, not a general aspiration
📚 Research basis

SDT (Deci & Ryan) confirms that feedback aligned with autonomy and competence — rather than control — produces long-term behavior change. Coercive feedback produces short-term compliance and long-term disengagement.

⚕️
Clinical parallel: In surgical teams, specific behavioral feedback after procedures reduces adverse event rates. The same mechanism applies in your chapter: vague feedback cannot produce specific change. Specificity is not harsh — it's respectful. It tells people exactly what to work with.
Domain 5 · CONNECT Pillar

Run a Meeting That Works

Bad meetings don't just waste time — they signal that the leader doesn't value the team's time. A well-run 30-minute chapter meeting builds more trust than a chaotic 90-minute one. Here's the evidence-based structure.

45-Minute Chapter Meeting Protocol
0–5
Check-in (Relational)
One word or one sentence — how is everyone arriving? This 5-minute investment predicts participation quality for the next 40 minutes.
5–10
Wins & Recognition
Specific acknowledgments of what went well. Public recognition activates dopamine and signals what behavior is valued.
10–30
Agenda Items (Prepared)
Agenda sent 24 hours before. Owner identified for each item. Decisions documented in real time.
30–40
Open Discussion
"What's on your mind that we haven't addressed?" Protects informal communication that gets buried in structure.
40–45
Close & Commitments
Each person states one specific action before the next meeting. Named commitments are 3× more likely to be completed than general intentions (Gollwitzer, 1999).
📋
Meeting quality test: After a meeting, ask privately: "Did everyone who had something to say get a chance to say it?" If not, your meeting structure is serving the loudest voices, not the room. Adjust accordingly.

Domain 6 · CONNECT Pillar

Event Planning Fundamentals

Every successful chapter event follows the same three-phase arc. The difference between a good event and a great one is almost always in the pre-event work, not the day itself.

Phase 1 · 2–4 Weeks Before
Plan
1
Define success — what does "it went well" look like specifically?
2
Identify all roles — who owns each piece?
3
Build a run-of-show timeline
4
Identify what can go wrong — and prepare for the top 3
Phase 2 · Day Of
Execute
1
Brief your team 30 min before — roles, timing, escalation plan
2
Empower team to make small decisions — you shouldn't be solving everything
3
Observe and adapt — stick to the plan until the plan needs to change
4
Visibly acknowledge good work in the moment
Phase 3 · Within 48 Hours
Debrief
1
What went well — specifically? (Don't skip this — it reinforces what to repeat)
2
What would we change next time?
3
Write it down — most learning evaporates within 72 hours without documentation
4
Thank every person who contributed — specifically
