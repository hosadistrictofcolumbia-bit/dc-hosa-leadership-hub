Domain 1 · BUILD Pillar

Influence Without Authority

You cannot order a chapter to do anything. Your entire job is to get independent chapters across the District, dozens of advisors, and hundreds of members to move in a shared direction using influence alone. Cialdini's research on persuasion gives you the mechanisms.

🤝 Reciprocity

People feel obligated to return favors. Before asking a chapter for something, give first — share a resource, offer help, recognize their work publicly. The ask lands differently after genuine generosity.

📐 Consistency

People act consistently with prior commitments. Get chapters to state a small commitment early ("Will you commit to sending one rep to the kickoff?") — it makes the larger ask easier later.

👥 Social Proof

People look to others for cues on what to do. "Three chapters have already signed up" is more persuasive than any argument about why they should participate. Visibility of early adopters drives the rest.

❤️ Liking

People say yes to people they like and trust. Build genuine relationships with advisors and chapter officers before you need something from them — not transactionally, but because the relationship matters.

🎓 Authority

People defer to credible expertise. Your authority as SEC doesn't come from title — it comes from being visibly competent, prepared, and knowledgeable about what you're asking chapters to do.

⏳ Scarcity

People value what's limited. "Spots for the leadership training are capped at 30" creates urgency that "sign up whenever" never will. Use this honestly, not manipulatively.

📚 Research basis

Cialdini's Influence (1984, updated 2021) identified these six principles through decades of behavioral research — and they remain the most validated framework for ethical persuasion. The key word is ethical: these principles work because they're rooted in genuine human psychology, not manipulation. Used with integrity, they're the difference between an SEC that struggles to get chapter buy-in and one that consistently mobilizes the affiliate.

Domain 2 · EMPOWER Pillar

Motivation Lab — Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Herzberg discovered something counterintuitive: the things that cause dissatisfaction and the things that cause satisfaction are different factors entirely — fixing one doesn't automatically create the other. This changes how you think about motivating chapters and advisors statewide.

Hygiene Factors

Prevent dissatisfaction — but don't create motivation on their own
Clear communication and reliable logistics
Fair policies applied consistently across chapters
Reasonable deadlines and advance notice
Basic administrative support (forms, access, resources)
Timely responses to chapter questions

Motivator Factors

Actually create engagement and drive
Recognition — public, specific, timely
Meaningful work connected to a larger mission
Growth opportunities and increasing responsibility
Achievement — visible progress and milestones
Genuine involvement in shaping decisions
💡
The SEC application: If a chapter isn't engaging, first check hygiene factors — are they getting timely responses? Clear information? If hygiene is solid but engagement is still low, the problem is motivators — they need recognition, meaning, or ownership, not just smoother logistics. Fixing hygiene issues alone will never create genuine enthusiasm — it only removes friction.
📚 Research basis

Herzberg's two-factor theory (1959) emerged from studying what actually drove engagement versus what merely prevented disengagement — and found they were structurally different. This distinction is still cited in organizational psychology because it explains a common leadership mistake: assuming that removing pain points will automatically create motivation. It won't. Motivation requires its own deliberate design.

Domain 3 · ADVANCE Pillar

Force Field Analysis for Change

Every statewide initiative faces forces pushing it forward and forces holding it back. Lewin's Force Field Analysis — grounded in the physics concept of homeostasis, where systems resist change to maintain equilibrium — gives you a structured way to see both and act strategically.

Example: Getting Every Chapter to Submit Officer Applications On Time
Map the driving forces (push toward change) against restraining forces (push against it). Strategy: strengthen driving forces AND reduce restraining forces — don't just push harder.
✓ Driving Forces
SEC visibility and reminders
Peer chapters submitting early (social proof)
Clear benefit of early submission (more planning time)
Advisor buy-in on the process
✗ Restraining Forces
Competing school deadlines at same time
Confusing or lengthy application form
Advisor turnover / new advisors unfamiliar with process
No clear consequence for lateness historically
📚 Research basis

Lewin's model (1951) draws on the concept of homeostasis — systems naturally resist change to maintain stable equilibrium, and this resistance is not irrational, it's the restraining forces doing exactly what they evolved to do. The strategic insight: increasing driving force alone often increases restraining force too (pushing harder creates more pushback). The more sustainable path is often to reduce restraining forces — simplify the form, clarify the process — rather than simply pushing louder for compliance.

Domain 4 · CONNECT Pillar

Coalition Building

Chapters across the District have different cultures, capacities, and advisor relationships. Moving all of them in the same direction requires strategic sequencing, not a single blanket announcement.

🌱

Early Adopter Strategy

Not every chapter needs the same approach at the same time — sequence matters more than most SEC teams realize.

1
Identify 2-3 chapters most likely to adopt quickly (strong advisor relationship, engaged officers)
2
Give them early access, extra support, and make their early success visible
3
Use their momentum as social proof for the broader rollout

Domain 5 · Applied

Running a Statewide Initiative

Bringing everything together: the four-phase arc every successful SEC initiative follows, from Spark One to a new competitive event push to a service campaign.

Phase 1
Design with Force Field in Mind
Map driving and restraining forces before finalizing the plan
Simplify wherever a restraining force is process friction
Identify hygiene vs. motivator needs for chapter buy-in
Phase 2
Sequence with Early Adopters
Launch with 2-3 chapters most likely to succeed quickly
Give them extra support and make early wins visible
Use momentum as social proof for the wider rollout
Phase 3
Scale with Influence Principles
Use reciprocity — give resources before asking for commitment
Use consistency — get small commitments before the big ask
Adapt approach per advisor relationship strength
Phase 4
Sustain with Motivators
Public, specific recognition for chapters that engaged
Connect the initiative back to DC HOSA's mission explicitly
Document what worked for the next SEC cohort
