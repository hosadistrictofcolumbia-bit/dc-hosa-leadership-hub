Domain 1

Know Yourself

Leadership starts with self-understanding. Before you lead others, you need a clear picture of how you think, feel, and respond — and the neuroscience behind why that matters.

🧠

Emotional Intelligence (EQ)

Daniel Goleman · Why EQ predicts leadership success more reliably than IQ — and the neuroscience that explains it

1
Self-Awareness — Recognizing your own emotions as they happen
2
Self-Regulation — Managing your responses, not just suppressing feelings
3
Empathy — Understanding others' emotional states accurately
4
Social Skills — Managing relationships and inspiring others
📚 Research basis

When a strong emotion hits, the amygdala — your brain's threat detector — can hijack the prefrontal cortex before conscious thought kicks in. This "amygdala hijack" (Goleman, 1995) means you literally cannot access your best thinking when emotionally flooded. Self-regulation isn't willpower — it's building the neural habit of pausing long enough for the prefrontal cortex to re-engage. EQ is trainable: regular reflection practice literally thickens the prefrontal cortex over time (Davidson et al., 2012).

Growth vs. Fixed Mindset

Carol Dweck · The belief system that determines whether you learn from failure — and the brain science behind it

Growth ✓
Abilities can develop
Challenges = opportunity
Feedback is data
Fixed ✗
Abilities are static
Challenges feel threatening
Feedback feels like attack
📚 Research basis

Neuroplasticity research (Kolb & Whishaw, 2009) confirms that the brain forms new synaptic connections every time you learn something new — your brain is literally physically different after learning. Dweck's longitudinal studies showed that students taught growth mindset showed measurably greater academic gains than control groups. The most important word: "yet." "I'm not good at this yet" activates a completely different neural response than "I'm not good at this."

Domain 3

Build Confidence

Confidence is a skill, not a trait. Bandura's self-efficacy research shows that four specific sources build self-belief — mastery experiences, vicarious learning, verbal encouragement, and physiological state management. These four quadrants map directly to those sources.

🪞 Self-Awareness (Bandura: Physiological State)

📔 Journal for 30 days — track moods and triggers, look for patterns
🧘 5-minute guided breathing before high-stakes moments — slows heart rate, signals safety to amygdala
🤝 Ask a trusted person for feedback on one thing you want to improve
📋 List 3 challenges you faced this week and what each taught you

⭐ Self-Belief (Bandura: Mastery Experiences)

📝 List your top 5 strengths and review them before difficult moments
🧗 Try one new challenging thing per month — completing it is evidence to your brain that you can
✉️ Write a letter to your future self about your growth right now
🎯 Set one bold goal this quarter. Track small daily steps — each step is a mastery experience.

🎤 Self-Expression (Bandura: Verbal Encouragement)

📣 Speak up at least once in every group this week — one sentence counts
🎙️ Record a voice memo about your goals — hearing yourself articulate them builds commitment
✍️ Write a thank-you note and deliver it in person — giving recognition builds your own confidence too

❤️ Self-Care (Bandura: Physiological State)

📅 Schedule recovery time — confidence erodes when the body is depleted
🌿 20 minutes in nature without a phone — cortisol reduction is measurable within this window
🌙 Create a consistent sleep routine — memory consolidation (and self-regulation) depend on it
🌱
The Compound Effect (Bandura, 1997): Self-efficacy builds through repeated small successes — not single dramatic moments. Pick one action per quadrant this week. Each completed action is neurological evidence that you are capable, and that evidence accumulates.

Domain 6

Learn How to Learn

Healthcare providers are lifelong learners — continuous learning is a professional requirement, not a personal choice. These six methods are backed by cognitive science and used in medical education programs worldwide.

🃏 Leitner System

Organize flashcards by how well you know each concept. Review difficult ones daily, easy ones weekly. The system forces you to spend time where you have actual gaps — not where you feel comfortable.

Research basis: Spaced repetition exploits the "spacing effect" (Ebbinghaus, 1885) — information reviewed at increasing intervals is retained exponentially longer than information crammed. Medical students using spaced repetition score 25% higher on licensing exams.

📚 SQ3R Method

Survey → Question → Read → Recite → Review. Skim structure first. Create questions from headings. Read to find answers. Summarize from memory. Return to check.

Research basis: Active questioning before reading creates retrieval pathways (Pressley et al., 1992). The brain learns by attaching new information to questions it already wants answered — passive reading skips this step.

📝 Cornell Notes

Divide your page: Key Points column (left), Details (right), Summary (bottom). Review by covering details and recalling from key points alone. Rebuild the content from prompts.

Research basis: The Testing Effect (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006) shows that retrieving information from memory strengthens retention far more than re-reading the same content. Cornell Notes structure forces retrieval practice into every review session.

🎨 Dual Coding

Combine words with visuals — diagrams, mind maps, annotated illustrations. Information encoded in two formats (verbal + visual) is recalled more easily than information encoded one way.

Research basis: Paivio's Dual Coding Theory (1971) demonstrated that verbal and visual memory systems are independent — activating both simultaneously dramatically increases retention and recall speed.

🗺️ Method of Loci

Visualize a familiar route. Place pieces of information at specific landmarks. Recall by mentally walking the route. Works for lists, sequences, physiological processes.

Research basis: Memory champions use this technique universally. It works because the hippocampus — your spatial memory system — is one of the brain's most powerful storage mechanisms. You hijack navigation memory to carry other information.

🧩 Feynman Technique

Explain any concept as if teaching someone with no background. Find every gap in your explanation — those gaps are gaps in your understanding. Fill them. Repeat until the explanation is complete and simple.

Research basis: The "generation effect" (Slamecka & Graf, 1978) shows that information you generate yourself is remembered far better than information you receive. If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it well enough to use it clinically.
🏥
Healthcare parallel: Pennebaker's expressive writing research (1997) showed that structured written reflection — just 15–20 minutes — measurably improved immune function and reduced physician burnout. Reflection isn't soft skill work: it's a clinical resilience practice.
