Ask Me 3

Lead YourselfCommunityCONNECT10 minCE: Healthy Living

Three questions, asked of any provider at any appointment, turn a visit you sit through into one you understand. It is the most transferable thing in this domain — a member uses it at their own appointment, teaches it to a parent, and hands it out at a community table.

The three
  • What is my main problem?
  • What do I need to do?
  • Why is it important for me to do this?
How to use it
  • Ask them out loud. Providers are trained to answer them and most are relieved to be asked.
  • If the answer to the second one has more than three parts, ask which one comes first.
  • Write the answers down before you leave the room. Not after.
Teach it to one person

The card is not learned until you have handed it to someone who will use it — a parent, a grandparent, a younger sibling. That is also what the Healthy Living portfolio scores: the ability to explain a health decision to someone else.

Do this
  1. Print the wallet card. Put it in your phone case.
  2. Use it at your next appointment, or role-play it with a chapter partner in five minutes.
  3. Give one card to one family member and explain why.

Where it shows up: Every appointment you or your family attend. Healthy Living Round Two, where explaining a personal health decision is directly scored.

Coach note
Have members practise this on each other before they take it to a real appointment. The awkwardness is in saying it aloud the first time, not in the questions.

Read It, Use It

Lead YourselfCommunityEMPOWER15 min

Health literacy starts with the basic version: can you read a label, a dosage instruction, an appointment letter, and do what it says? Most members assume they can. Many cannot, on the documents that matter, and nobody has ever checked.

The three levels, and which one this is

Health literacy is not one skill. It rises in three steps, and this card is the first.

  • Functional — reading and applying basic health information. A label, a dose, a notice. This card.
  • Interactive — extracting information and acting on it with other people. Asking a provider, helping a family member navigate a system, explaining something accurately to a peer. Lead a Team.
  • Critical — analysing information and using it to change the conditions that produce health. Why outcomes differ by ward, and what would move them. Lead Beyond.
Four documents to practise on
  • An over-the-counter medicine label. Find the dose for your age, the maximum in 24 hours, and the one thing it says not to take it with.
  • A prescription label. Find how many, how often, with or without food, and the refill count.
  • An appointment letter. Find the date, the place, what to bring, and what happens if you miss it.
  • A vaccine information sheet. Find what it is for, the common side effects, and who to call.
Do this
  1. Take the four documents to a chapter meeting. In pairs, find each item and time it.
  2. Note which one took longest. That is the document type the chapter should make a handout for.
  3. Move to Lead a Team when you can do all four in under two minutes each.

Where it shows up: Your own life first. Then the chapter’s health fair table, where the same documents are what people bring you.

Coach note
This looks too basic to bother with. Run it once anyway. The members who struggle with the prescription label are the ones who will never say so, and they are the ones who most need the next card.

Source Check

Lead YourselfCommunityCONNECT10 minCE: Health Literacy Ambassador

Most health claims a member meets are in a feed, not a library. A four-question filter applied in thirty seconds is worth more than any lecture on misinformation, because it works at the moment the claim appears.

Four questions
  • Who published this? A person, an organisation, a company? Can you find them outside this post?
  • When? Health guidance changes. A date is not optional.
  • What are they selling? A product, a programme, a subscription, an outrage. If you cannot tell, that is the answer.
  • Does MedlinePlus agree? Search the topic there. If it is not there, or says something different, the claim needs more than one source before you repeat it.
MedlinePlus as the cross-reference

Free, plain-language, from the National Library of Medicine, and citable. It is also the reference source for the Health Literacy Ambassador programme, so learning to reach for it does double work.

What to do with a claim that fails

Do not repost. Do not argue in the comments. If it matters, take it to a chapter meeting as a Source Check example — that is a five-minute segment with no preparation.

Do this
  1. Take the last health claim you saw in a feed. Run the four questions. Time it.
  2. Find the same topic on MedlinePlus. Note what it says differently.
  3. Bring one failed claim to the next chapter meeting.

Where it shows up: Any feed, any day. Health Literacy Ambassador, which exists to counter health misinformation and uses MedlinePlus as its anchor.

Coach note
Do not run this as a lesson about misinformation. Run it as a game — bring three claims, the chapter scores them. The filter is learned by using it, and the members who learn it teach their families.

Quorum and the Order of Business

Lead YourselfGovernanceBUILD15 minCE: Organizational Leadership · Parliamentary Procedure · 24%

Two things decide whether a meeting can act at all and what it does first. A chapter that skips them makes decisions that can be challenged later, and wastes the first twenty minutes every time.

Quorum

The minimum number of members who must be present for the chapter to conduct business. It is set in the chapter’s bylaws — if yours do not say, the default under RONR is a majority of members. Without quorum a meeting can hear reports and set a next date; it cannot decide anything.

  • The chair confirms quorum at the start, out loud, and the secretary records it.
  • If members leave and quorum is lost, business stops. Anything decided after that point can be challenged.
  • Know your number. If your chapter has never checked its bylaws for this, that is the first action.
The standard order of business

RONR’s default sequence. A chapter may adopt its own, but this is what an unfamiliar chair should reach for.

  • Reading and approval of minutes
  • Reports of officers, boards and standing committees
  • Reports of special committees
  • Special orders — business previously scheduled for this meeting
  • Unfinished business and general orders — anything carried over
  • New business
Do this
  1. Find the quorum number in your chapter bylaws. If there is no bylaws document, that is a Lead Beyond problem — flag it to your advisor now.
  2. Put the six-line order of business at the top of the next agenda, verbatim.
  3. Have the chair state quorum before the first item, every meeting, until it is a habit.

Where it shows up: The first two minutes of every meeting. Also a direct test item in the Organizational Leadership parliamentary band.

Coach note
Chapters that ‘never seem to get through the agenda’ almost always lack an order of business, not time. Ask to see the last three agendas before offering any other advice.

Robert’s Rules of Order Newly Revised In Brief, 3rd ed.; RONR 12th ed. §3 (quorum), §41 (order of business).

The Five Moves

Lead YourselfGovernanceBUILD20 minCE: Organizational Leadership · Parliamentary Procedure · 24%

Almost everything a chapter decides can be handled with five moves. Learn those five, use them on one real decision at every meeting, and you can function in any deliberative body — including the ILC Business Session — without ever memorising the rest of the book.

The five, in order
  • Move it — “I move that…” One sentence, stated as the thing you want the chapter to do.
  • Second it — a second means one other person thinks it is worth discussing. It is not agreement.
  • Debate it — the chair recognises speakers; each speaker addresses the chair; nobody speaks twice until everyone who wants to has spoken once.
  • Amend it — insert words, strike words, or strike and insert. An amendment is voted on before the motion it changes.
  • Vote it — a voice vote first. If the result is in doubt, anyone may call for a division, which is a show of hands or standing count.
What each move is for

The test asks purpose, not vocabulary. A move you can name but cannot place is not yet learned.

  • A motion exists so the chapter debates one clear proposal rather than a topic.
  • A second exists so one person cannot consume the meeting alone.
  • Debate rules exist so the quietest member gets a turn before the loudest gets a second one.
  • Amendment exists so a nearly-right proposal gets fixed instead of killed.
  • Division exists so a close vote is counted rather than guessed.
Do this
  1. At the next chapter meeting, take one decision the chapter genuinely needs to make and run it using only these five moves. Not a simulation — a real decision.
  2. Print the card. Keep it at the officer table for the first three meetings, then take it away.
  3. Once a month, ask a member at random to name the five moves in order. Spacing, not review, is what makes it stick.

Where it shows up: Every chapter business meeting. You will already be ready for the Parliamentary Procedure band of Organizational Leadership, and for the floor at ILC.

Coach note
The instinct is to teach the whole procedure in September. Do not. Five moves, retrieved monthly on real business, outperforms one comprehensive workshop that nobody retains by October. If a member asks about a motion not on this card, that is the signal to move them to Lead a Team, not to expand this card.

Who Governs What

Lead YourselfGovernanceBUILD20 minCE: Organizational Leadership · HOSA Inc. + Structure · 28%

‘HOSA says’ is not a sentence. There are four levels, each decides different things, and most of the confusion in a chapter — who to ask, who can change a rule, why a deadline in DC differs from the one on hosa.org — comes from not holding the map. This card is the map.

The four levels
  • HOSA Inc. — the corporate entity. A Board of Directors and a Headquarters staff. Sets the competitive event guidelines, the Bylaws, affiliation, and ILC. Not a student body.
  • International Executive Council — the elected student officers of International HOSA. Represent members, run the ILC business session, do not set policy on their own.
  • Chartered Association — DC HOSA. The State Advisor, state staff, and the State Executive Council. Runs the state calendar, SLC, cut scores, the DC internal ILC deadline, and any DC divergence from the international model.
  • Local Chapter — your school. The chapter advisor, chapter officers, and members. Decides its own meetings, projects, events entered, and whether it affiliates.
What each cannot decide

The part that prevents most mistakes.

  • A Local Chapter cannot change a competitive event rule or a state deadline. Ask; do not assume.
  • The State Executive Council cannot change the DC HOSA Bylaws or Standing Rules on its own — those change by the process written into them.
  • The International Executive Council does not write the event guidelines. HOSA Inc. does.
  • DC HOSA can set an earlier deadline than International HOSA, and does — the DC internal ILC deadline is earlier than the international one so the State Advisor can review every submission while problems are still fixable.
The words

Precision here is not pedantry; it is how you know which level you are talking to.

  • International HOSA, never “national HOSA” or “nationals”.
  • ILC — International Leadership Conference. There is no NLC.
  • Chartered Association, not “state chapter”. Local Chapter, not “club”.
  • State Executive Council or SEC, not “state board”.
Do this
  1. Take the last three questions your chapter sent up the chain. For each, name the level that actually decides it.
  2. Put the four-level map on the wall at your next officer meeting and leave it there.
  3. Before the next “can we…” question leaves the chapter, check which level owns it.

Where it shows up: Every time a question leaves the chapter. It is also the HOSA Inc. and Organizational Structure bands of Organizational Leadership together — 28% of that test.

Coach note
When an officer uses “nationals” or “HOSA said”, do not correct the word — ask which of the four levels they mean. The word fixes itself once the map is held.

HOSA Handbook Sections A, B and C; HOSA Bylaws (current edition at hosa.org). DC divergences per DC-FACTS.md. Structure drawn from the hosa.org website is volatile — date anything taken from it.

A Goal That Survives Contact

Lead YourselfSelfEMPOWER15 minCE: Healthy Living

Specific, hard goals beat ‘do your best’. That much is settled. But a well-formed goal is not an executed one, and the thing that closes the gap between intending and doing is naming the obstacle in advance and deciding — before it appears — exactly what you will do when it does.

Four lines on a card
  • Wish — one sentence. What you want to do this term.
  • Outcome — what it looks like when it is done. Be concrete enough to picture.
  • Obstacle — the internal one. Not ‘no time’; the thing in you that will get in the way. Usually a habit, a fear, or a Tuesday.
  • Plan — an if-then. ‘If [the obstacle shows up], then I will [specific action].’
Why the obstacle line is the whole card

Positive thinking about the outcome on its own reduces the energy to pursue it. Naming the obstacle, right after picturing the outcome, is what produces the plan. The if-then is what makes the plan fire without deciding in the moment.

Not SMART

SMART produces well-formed goals. This produces executed ones. If the chapter has a SMART worksheet, replace it with this.

Do this
  1. Fill the four lines for one goal this term. Wallet-size.
  2. Read the if-then aloud. If it does not name a specific action you could do in under two minutes, rewrite it.
  3. Check the card at the January reset.

Where it shows up: The start of every term. The Two-Competency plan is two of these. Healthy Living’s goal-setting is one of these with a health topic.

Coach note
When a member’s goal fails, ask to see the obstacle line. It is nearly always missing or external. Rewrite that line with them and the goal usually works the second time.
Grounded in
RMental contrasting with implementation intentions — WOOP
Oettingen, mental contrasting; Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006) meta-analysis of implementation intentions, effect around d = .65. Goal specificity per Locke & Latham (R). SMART is the practitioner packaging of the latter (P).

Find Your Event

Lead YourselfSelfADVANCE20 min

The most common reason a member drifts away after SLC is that they were assigned an event rather than fitted to one. A free federal interest inventory plus a short crosswalk routes a member to the competitive event categories that match how they actually like to work — before anyone signs them up for anything.

Take the profiler

The O*NET Interest Profiler, from the U.S. Department of Labor. No account, no cost, results on screen. It scores six interest types.

The crosswalk

Take your top two types and read across.

  • Investigative → Health Science events and the Academic Testing Center. You like finding out.
  • Social → Health Professions and the Leadership category. You like people and helping.
  • Realistic → Emergency Preparedness and the skills-based events. You like doing with your hands.
  • Enterprising → Teamwork events and the advocacy-shaped Leadership events. You like persuading and organising.
  • Artistic → Health Career Photography, Public Service Announcement, the presentation-heavy events.
  • Conventional → the Academic Testing Center, Medical Terminology, the events with a defined body of knowledge.
Then go to the Study Hub

Once you have a category, the Study Hub has a tool for every event DC HOSA offers. This card gets you to the right shelf. It does not prepare you for the test — that is the Study Hub’s job, and it stays there.

Do this
  1. Take the Interest Profiler. Note your top two types.
  2. Read across the crosswalk. List three events that fit.
  3. Take those three to your advisor before registration closes in January.

Where it shows up: Before event selection every year. The crosswalk is the same one the Middle School Division uses in its careers-to-events segment — built once, used in both places.

Coach note
Advisors assign events by what the chapter needs entered. Ask instead what the profiler said. A member in the right category competes for three years; a member in the wrong one competes once.

Know Your Strengths

Lead YourselfSelfEMPOWER25 min

Most strengths tools give you a label. One gives you a list of sixty specific things a student leader can do, sorted into eight clusters, with a free inventory built for young people. A list that specific is something you can write a goal against. A label is not.

The eight clusters

Sixty competencies sit inside these. Each one is defined on four dimensions — do you know it, do you value it, can you do it, do you actually do it.

  • Learning and reasoning · Self-awareness and development · Group dynamics · Interpersonal interaction · Civic responsibility · Communication · Strategic planning · Personal behaviour
Take the inventory
  • The Student Leadership Competencies Inventory is free, has a shortened youth version, and takes about twenty minutes.
  • Results are not stored. Print or screenshot them before closing the page — you will need them in April.
  • A separate free 360 evaluation measures the behaviour dimension — what others see you do. That one comes later.
What to do with sixty

Nothing, yet. The point of this card is the vocabulary. The next card picks two.

Why this maps to the pillars

The eight clusters sit cleanly under BUILD, EMPOWER, ADVANCE and CONNECT. That gives every card in this Hub and every module in the Advisor Development Program a common language.

Do this
  1. Take the youth inventory. Save the results somewhere you will find in April.
  2. Read the eight cluster names aloud. Pick the one that surprised you.
  3. Move to Two Competencies, One Term when you have the results in hand.

Where it shows up: September, for every member. It is the measurement spine’s first instrument — September and April.

Coach note

Not Yet

Lead YourselfSelfEMPOWER10 min

How you explain a setback to yourself decides what you do next. Treating it as a fixed limit closes the door. Treating it as a current state with a trajectory keeps it open, and the difference often comes down to one word.

The word

"I'm not good at this yet." The word yet frames a current state with an implied path. Without it, the sentence is a verdict.

Where it shows up in HOSA
  • Not placing at a competition — "I'm just not talented" versus "what specifically can I work on before the next one?"
  • When feedback stings — hearing "you're not good enough" versus hearing a specific path to better.
  • When someone else succeeds — feeling threatened versus getting curious about how they got there.
Do this
  1. Finish this sentence honestly: "I'm not good at ___ yet."
  2. Write the one thing you are actively doing to build it. If nothing, that is the real finding.

Where it shows up: After any competition result, and in the first officer debrief of the year.

Coach note
Do not use this to talk a member out of disappointment. Let the disappointment be real first — the reframe only works after it has been heard.
Grounded in
RMindset — Carol Dweck
Dweck, Mindset: The New Psychology of Success.
Large replication studies have found growth-mindset interventions produce small average effects, smaller than the original work suggested. Present it as a useful reframe, not as a reliable performance lever.

Reflect in Three Questions

Lead YourselfSelfEMPOWER10 min

Reflection only happens if the structure is short enough to actually use. Three questions, three words each, and it works after a competition, after a meeting, after a week. It is the one reflection structure across this whole Hub, so that a member does not learn four competing ones.

The three
  • What? — what happened. Facts only. What you did, what others did, what the result was.
  • So what? — why it matters. What it tells you about yourself, the team, the plan.
  • Now what? — what you will do differently, and when.
Wins and losses, same three questions

After a win, the So What is usually ‘what specifically made that work, so I can do it on purpose’. After a loss, the So What is usually ‘what was in my control’. The Now What is the same either way — one change, one date.

The healthcare parallel

Every clinical role debriefs. Surgery does it after every case; emergency medicine after every shift. The habit you build here on a competition result is the one you will use on a patient outcome.

Do this
  1. After the next event, write the three answers in under five minutes.
  2. Keep them somewhere you can find. Six of these across a year is a portfolio.
  3. Bring the Now What to your next chapter meeting.

Where it shows up: After any competition, event, or leadership moment. Inside the Debrief at Team. It is what the Wins & Losses journal became.

Coach note
Members overwrite the What with the So What — ‘I was nervous and it went badly’. Send it back once: what happened, in facts, no feelings. The reflection gets sharper immediately.
Grounded in

Why You Show Up

Lead YourselfSelfEMPOWER20 min

People do not keep showing up because they were told to. Three things keep them coming back — having some say in what they do, feeling like they are getting better at it, and belonging to the people doing it. Take any one away and attendance drops, whatever the programming looks like.

Autonomy — having some say

Ask who wants to own something before assigning it. Share the goal and the constraints, then leave the method to them.

  • "I'd like someone to lead the community service planning — who wants to own this, and what would you need?" rather than "Alex, you're doing community service planning."
  • "What do you think we should do here?" before "Here's what I think we should do."
  • Kills it: micromanaging a task you delegated, overriding a decision without explaining why, handing out yes/no jobs that are never real ownership.
Competence — getting visibly better

Calibrate the task just above where someone currently is. Too easy and they drift; too hard and they shut down.

  • Stretch assignments for members who have mastered their current role — with the support said out loud.
  • Break big tasks down for new members. Small wins are what build the base.
  • Name specific growth in public: "I want everyone to know how that was handled this week — that was not easy, and it was excellent."
Relatedness — belonging to the people

Members who feel they belong show up, contribute, and recruit. Members who do not quietly disappear.

  • Learn every member's name and one specific thing about them within three meetings.
  • Connect members to each other, not only to you: "Have you met — you two should talk about this."
  • Make the mission concrete: name the ward, the event, the people reached. Specificity beats abstraction.
  • Missing two consecutive meetings with no contact is a belonging signal, not an attendance one. Reach out personally within 48 hours.
Do this
  1. Pick one member who has been doing the same task for more than two months. Decide whether they are still growing.
  2. Look at your last three chapter decisions and count how much of the input was yours versus the team's.
  3. Transfer one decision to a member this week, and say what the constraints are rather than how to do it.

Where it shows up: Officer team meetings, and the recruitment conversation in the first month of the year.

Coach note
Officers hear this as three things to add. It is usually one thing to stop. Ask which of the three they already do well, and protect that before adding another.

Deci & Ryan. Relatedness section draws on Baumeister & Leary (1995); the challenge-skill calibration draws on Csikszentmihalyi.

Say the Uncomfortable Thing

Lead YourselfTeamEMPOWER10 min

Teams that work are ones where people can say ‘I think this is wrong’, ‘I don’t know how to do this’, or ‘I made a mistake’ without paying for it. That condition has a name, it is the strongest single predictor of team effectiveness there is, and it is created by what the person in charge does — not by team-building activities.

What it is

Psychological safety: the shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Not niceness. Not the absence of disagreement. The opposite — a team where disagreement is cheap enough to happen.

As a member, three things you can do
  • Ask the question everyone is thinking. The first person to say ‘I don’t understand the plan’ makes it possible for the next four.
  • Report your own mistake before it is found. Early and plainly. ‘I dropped this, here is where it stands.’
  • When someone else does either of those, respond to the content and not to the courage. Treat it as normal, because it should be.
How the chapter measures it

There is a seven-item scale, free and short. Officers run it in October and again in March and report the team average — never individual answers. If the number is low, the fix is in what the officers do, not in the members.

Do this
  1. At the next meeting, ask one question you would normally keep to yourself.
  2. The next time you drop something, say so in the group chat within the day.
  3. Answer the seven-item pulse honestly when the officers send it.

Where it shows up: Every meeting, every group chat. The Debrief card depends on it entirely.

Coach note
You cannot instruct a team to be safe. You can only go first — state your own mistake before anyone else speaks, respond to a hard question with thanks rather than defence, and do it every time. The seven-item pulse tells you whether it is working; the members’ behaviour tells you whether you are.
Grounded in
RPsychological safety — Edmondson
Edmondson (1999), Administrative Science Quarterly — the construct and the seven-item team psychological safety scale. Identified as the strongest predictor of team effectiveness in Google’s Project Aristotle.

The Debrief

Lead YourselfTeamBUILD20 min

Experience on its own does not teach. A competition where everyone performs and then moves on produces far less than the same competition followed by twenty structured minutes. Most of the learning from a HOSA event is in the debrief, not the event.

Timing

Three windows, each doing a different job.

  • Within 24 hours — how did it feel. This has to happen while the feeling is still fresh.
  • Within 48 hours — what worked, what changes, what the specific next step is.
  • Within a week — each participant writes their own reflection down.
Who runs it

The person leading facilitates rather than evaluates. Ask questions, name what you observed, and hold back recommendations until after the group has done its own analysis.

Do this
  1. Book the 48-hour slot before the event happens, not after.
  2. Open by stating one thing you personally got wrong, before anyone else speaks.
  3. Close with one named change, one owner, and one date.

Where it shows up: After SLC, after any chapter event, and after an officer team misses something.

Coach note

When Someone Is Upset

Lead YourselfTeamCONNECT10 min

Saying the wrong thing to someone in distress does not just fail to help — it tells them their experience is invalid, and trust closes in seconds. In a clinic that costs a patient relationship; in a chapter it costs a member. These are questions that open the conversation instead of closing it.

Ask this, not that
  • Anger — ask ‘what boundary do you feel was crossed?’ Not ‘calm down’ or ‘it’s not a big deal’.
  • Sadness — ask ‘what loss or unmet expectation is this?’ Not ‘it could be worse’.
  • Anxiety — ask ‘what worries you most, and what is actually in your control?’ Not ‘just relax’.
  • Frustration — ask ‘what is at stake that you care about?’ Not ‘let it go’.
  • Overwhelm — ask ‘what is one thing we could take off your plate right now?’ Not ‘everyone’s busy’.
  • Shame — ask ‘what story are you telling yourself about what happened?’ Not ‘you shouldn’t feel that way’.
  • Fear — ask ‘what is the worst that could realistically happen, and could you handle it?’ Not ‘don’t be scared’.
  • Disappointment — ask ‘what were you expecting that didn’t happen?’ Not ‘you should have known’.
The pattern under all eight

Every ‘ask’ line is a question about the person’s experience. Every ‘not’ line is an instruction to stop having it. That is the whole difference, and it is enough.

Do this
  1. Pick the one emotion you most often meet in the chapter. Learn its ‘ask’ line by heart.
  2. The next time you catch yourself about to say a ‘not’ line, ask the question instead.
  3. Notice what happens in the following ten seconds.

Where it shows up: After a competition result. In the group chat at 11pm. At a health fair table when someone is scared of what they just read.

Coach note
This is a script, not a theory, and it is labelled as one. It works because it is specific and rehearsed. Do not let it become a poster — have members practise the lines in pairs once, out loud.

Nerves Are Fuel

Lead YourselfVoiceEMPOWER5 minCE: Prepared Speaking · Speaking Skills · Interviewing Skills

Telling yourself to calm down before you speak does not work, because calm and nervous are far apart and the body does not travel that distance in sixty seconds. Nervous and excited are almost the same state. Saying ‘I am excited’ out loud, once, outperformed trying to calm down — across speaking, singing, and maths tasks.

The one sentence

Out loud, before you walk in: ‘I am excited.’ Not ‘I am calm.’ Not ‘I am fine.’ The point is not to believe it. The point is that it reframes the racing heart as readiness rather than threat, and that changes what you do next.

What it replaces
  • Deep breathing to reach calm — works slowly, and you do not have slowly.
  • ‘Picture the audience in their underwear’ and the rest of the folklore.
  • The old build’s speaker model, which listed preparation, past success, self-esteem and support as the sources of confidence. All true, none of them available in the sixty seconds before a competitive event room. This is.
Then the exposure

Reappraisal is for the moment. Graduated exposure is for the year — the Speaking Ladder is the exposure schedule.

Do this
  1. Say it before the next thing you present. Out loud. Once.
  2. Notice what your body does with the same heart rate under the new label.
  3. Keep climbing the ladder — the reappraisal works better each time it is paired with a rung.

Where it shows up: The corridor outside any competitive event room. Before any presentation. Before testifying.

Coach note
Do not teach relaxation techniques for the sixty-second window. Teach this sentence, and reserve breathing and preparation for the weeks before. The member who says ‘I am excited’ walks in differently, and you can see it.

One Idea Per Slide

Lead YourselfVoiceEMPOWER10 minCE: Health Education · Public Service Announcement · Community Awareness

‘Your slides have too much text’ sounds like taste. It is not — it is a claim about how much a listener can process at once, and there is a mechanism behind it. Five principles, one line each, applied by the presenter before anyone else sees the deck.

The five-line check
  • Coherence — cut anything that does not serve the point. The decorative image, the third bullet, the logo on every slide.
  • Signalling — cue what matters. Bold the one number. Put the recommendation at the top.
  • Redundancy — do not read on-screen text aloud. If it is on the slide, say something else.
  • Modality — narrate the graphic; do not caption it. Words in the air plus a picture on the screen beats words on the screen plus the same words in the air.
  • Segmenting — one idea per slide, and let the listener control the pace where you can.
Why this version lands with students

Because it is a cognitive-load claim, not a design opinion. The listener has one channel for words and one for pictures; overload either and nothing gets through. That is a mechanism a health science student accepts.

The competitive-event variant

Each principle maps to a criterion on the presentation rating sheets — organisation, visual aids, delivery. Practice for the chapter and preparation for the event are the same activity.

Do this
  1. Run the five lines on your last deck. Score each one yes or no.
  2. Fix the worst one. Usually redundancy.
  3. Rung 3 of the Speaking Ladder is scored on this.

Where it shows up: Every presentation. Health Education, Public Service Announcement, Community Awareness, the Ambassador Level 1 presentation — all of them.

Coach note
When a member defends a crowded slide, ask which of the five principles it passes. The argument ends there, because it is no longer about preference.
Grounded in
RCognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning — Mayer
Mayer, Multimedia Learning (2nd ed., 2009) — coherence, signalling, redundancy, modality and segmenting principles; one of the most replicated bodies of instructional-design research.

The Speaking Ladder

Lead YourselfVoiceEMPOWER5 minCE: Prepared Speaking · Speaking Skills

Nobody gets better at speaking by speaking more. They get better by speaking against one specific criterion, with immediate feedback, and then doing it again. Four rungs, one per chapter meeting, each scored on exactly one thing. One criterion is what makes it practice rather than repetition.

Four rungs

Self-recorded on a phone. Scored against the one criterion only.

  • Rung 1 — a chapter announcement. Thirty seconds. Criterion: no filler words in the first sentence.
  • Rung 2 — a committee report. Two minutes. Criterion: opens with the recommendation, not the history.
  • Rung 3 — a topic presentation. Five minutes. Criterion: one idea per slide, eye contact across three zones of the room.
  • Rung 4 — a judged mock in the event format. Criterion: scored on the relevant competitive event rating sheet.
Why one criterion

Deliberate practice is targeted repetition against a specific standard with feedback, not general effort. A member told ‘be more confident’ has nothing to practise. A member told ‘no filler in the first sentence’ can hear on the recording whether they did it.

Where the rungs live

Rungs 1 and 2 are real business at real chapter meetings — the Five Moves and the committee report are already on the agenda. Rung 3 is a Health Literacy Ambassador Level 1 presentation. Rung 4 is the mock before SLC.

Do this
  1. Do rung 1 at the next meeting. Record it. Listen for the first sentence only.
  2. Do not move to rung 2 until rung 1 is clean twice.
  3. Officers: put a rung on every meeting agenda, rotating who climbs.

Where it shows up: Every chapter meeting. You will already be ready for Prepared Speaking and Speaking Skills, and for the presentation round of any event that has one.

Coach note
Resist giving more than one note. If the member’s first sentence was clean, say so and stop, even if the rest was rough. Next rung, next criterion. The whole method depends on the note count being one.
Grounded in
RDeliberate practice — Ericsson
Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993), Psychological Review — deliberate practice as targeted repetition against a specific criterion with immediate feedback.

  • As a behaviour-change and self-advocacy event.

Score It Before You Post It

Lead a TeamCommunityCONNECT15 minCE: Health Education · Public Service Announcement · Health Literacy Ambassador

Every flyer, post, poster and presentation a chapter makes is patient education material. There is a validated instrument that scores such material on two things — can people understand it, and can they act on it — and it takes ten minutes. It turns ‘is this a good flyer’ from a taste argument into a number.

Two scores
  • Understandability — can a person of any background work out the key messages? Plain words, short sentences, one idea at a time, a clear purpose stated up front, visuals that help rather than decorate.
  • Actionability — can they identify what to do? A specific action, stated as a verb, with the how and the when. ‘Get screened’ is not actionable. ‘Call this number to book a free screening on Saturday’ is.
The gate

Nothing the chapter produces goes out until it has been scored. Not as bureaucracy — as the one discipline that improves everything the chapter already makes without adding a single new activity.

  • Score it yourself first. Then have someone who did not make it score it. The gap between the two numbers is the finding.
  • Anything under the threshold you set gets one more pass. Usually the fix is cutting words and adding one sentence that starts with a verb.
  • Keep the scores. Six months in, the chapter can see whether its materials are getting better.
Do this
  1. Take the last thing your chapter published. Score it on the printed sheet.
  2. Rewrite the one sentence that would most raise the actionability score.
  3. Adopt the gate at the next officer meeting. Write it into the charter.

Where it shows up: Every chapter communication. Health Education, Public Service Announcement, Community Awareness — the rubrics for all three score exactly these two things. Every Level 2 Ambassador project.

Coach note
The first time a chapter scores its own flyer, the number is usually lower than they expect and the room goes quiet. Let it. Then ask what one change would move it most. That question is the whole tool.

Teach-Back

Lead a TeamCommunityEMPOWER5 minCE: Healthy Living · Health Education · Public Service Announcement

You do not know whether someone understood you until they say it back in their own words. Teach-back is a check on the explainer, not a quiz on the listener — which is why it is a leadership skill and not only a clinical one, and why five minutes of it at every meeting rehearses three competitive events at once.

The round

Five minutes of a chapter meeting. No slides.

  • One member explains a health concept in ninety seconds. Ask Me 3, a vaccine, why hydration matters — anything from MedlinePlus.
  • A second member explains it back, in their own words, to the first.
  • The first member revises the explanation based on what did not land. That revision is the learning.
The phrasing that makes it work
  • Not ‘do you understand?’ — everyone says yes.
  • ‘I want to make sure I explained that well. Can you tell me how you would describe it to someone else?’
  • If they cannot, the explanation failed, not the listener.
Do this
  1. Put a five-minute Teach-Back round on the next three agendas.
  2. Rotate who explains. Nobody explains twice until everyone has once.
  3. Keep a list of the concepts that took more than one revision — those are the chapter’s handout topics.

Where it shows up: Every chapter meeting. Healthy Living Round Two, Health Education and Public Service Announcement all score the ability to explain something so it lands.

Coach note
Watch for the explainer who blames the listener. Redirect, gently and every time: what would you change in what you said? That reframe is the entire point.

Chair a Meeting That Ends on Time

Lead a TeamGovernanceBUILD30 min

Most chapter meetings are not too long because there is too much to do. They are too long because the agenda is a list of nouns, nobody knows what a decision looks like, and the first two minutes were lost. All three are fixable before the meeting starts.

Agenda as questions

Rewrite every agenda line from a topic into the question the chapter has to answer.

  • “Fundraiser” becomes “Do we commit to a February fundraiser, and who leads it?”
  • “SLC” becomes “Which events are we entering, and by when do we need names?”
  • If a line cannot be turned into a question, it is an announcement. It goes in an email, not on the agenda. This one conversion removes about a third of most chapter agendas.
The first two minutes

How the chair opens sets the whole meeting. Research on workplace meetings finds the chair’s opening behaviour has a disproportionate effect on what follows.

  • Start at the stated time, even if the room is half full. The people who are on time are the ones you are rewarding.
  • State quorum, state the end time, state what the meeting must decide today.
  • Say one specific thing that went well since the last meeting, with a name attached.
The parking lot

A visible list for things raised that are not today’s question. Revisited before adjournment, never silently dropped.

  • Write it where everyone can see it, not in the chair’s notes.
  • In the last three minutes, read it aloud and assign each item to a date or a person.
  • An item parked three meetings running is a decision the chapter is avoiding. Name that.
Do this
  1. Take your next agenda and rewrite every line as a question. Delete the ones that cannot be.
  2. Write the opening — quorum, end time, the decisions — on the top of the page and read it.
  3. Put a parking lot on the whiteboard before anyone arrives.

Where it shows up: Every chapter meeting you chair. The written script is also what a chair uses in the Parliamentary Procedure team event.

Coach note
Watch the opening, not the middle. If a chair drifts into the first item without stating quorum, end time and today’s decisions, the meeting is already lost and no amount of agenda discipline recovers it.

Minutes, and the Decision Log

Lead a TeamGovernanceBUILD20 minCE: Parliamentary Procedure

Minutes and a decision log are two different documents that chapters keep trying to make into one. Minutes are the governance record — formal, RONR-shaped, and what the Parliamentary Procedure event asks you to produce. The decision log is working memory. Combining them produces minutes nobody reads and decisions nobody can find.

Minutes — what goes in

RONR minutes record what was done, not what was said.

  • Kind of meeting, name of the body, date, time, place; whether the chair and secretary were present; whether the previous minutes were approved.
  • Every main motion, exactly as adopted or as it stood when disposed of, with the name of the mover and the result.
  • Points of order and appeals, with the chair’s ruling.
  • Not: who said what in debate, the secretary’s opinion, or a summary of the discussion.
The decision log — one line each

Lives beside the minutes. Date, decision, who decided, why, and the date it should be revisited.

  • “14 Oct — February fundraiser is a bake sale, not a fun run. Officer team. Fun run needs a permit we cannot get by then. Revisit 1 Dec.”
  • Anyone can search it. Nobody has to read a whole meeting’s minutes to find out why the chapter stopped doing something in 2024.
  • The revisit date is what makes it a log rather than a list. A decision with no revisit date is a decision the chapter will relitigate.
Do this

One Motion a Month

Lead a TeamGovernanceBUILD15 minCE: Organizational Leadership · Parliamentary Procedure · 24%

Officers need more than the Five Moves, but they do not need all of it in September. One new motion class each month, used on real business that month, and by February the officer team can run any meeting the chapter will ever hold.

The drip

Each month adds one thing and uses it on one real item. An advisor can drop this into a chapter calendar unmodified.

  • September — the order of business; the chair’s written script; the quorum call. An officer chairs from the script.
  • October — Amend (including amending an amendment); Refer to committee. Refer one real decision to a committee.
  • November — Postpone to a certain time; Previous Question, which closes debate. Close debate properly on one item.
  • December — Minutes to RONR standard; the treasurer’s report. The secretary drafts minutes in competition format.
  • January — Precedence: why motions rank, and how to read the ladder. The chair handles a motion made out of order.
  • February — Committee reports, and the difference between a report and a recommendation. A committee reports and moves adoption.
What each motion is for

The Organizational Leadership test asks purpose. So does real life.

  • Amend — the proposal is nearly right. Fix it rather than kill it.
  • Refer to committee — the chapter does not have enough information to decide today. Send it to people who will get the information.
  • Postpone to a certain time — the chapter has the information but not the right people or moment. Name the meeting it comes back to.
  • Previous Question — everyone has been heard and the debate is now repeating. Requires two-thirds, because ending debate early is a serious thing to do to a minority.
  • Postpone Indefinitely — dispose of a motion the chapter does not want to vote on directly. The polite kill.
The cold-call

At the top of each officer meeting, four questions on the material from two months ago. Not last month. Two months. That gap is the intervention.

Do this
  1. Print the six-month table and put it in the chapter calendar today.
  2. Assign one officer to chair each month’s meeting from the script — rotate, so nobody hides behind the president.
  3. Start the cold-call in November. Four questions, October material. Keep it under three minutes.

Where it shows up: Officer meetings, September to February. By March the secretary’s December minutes are already a Parliamentary Procedure competition asset.

Coach note
The temptation is to skip ahead when a keen officer asks. Resist it — the schedule is the point. If they want more, point them at Precedence in Lead Beyond and let them read ahead on their own time.

Virtual Meetings That Do Not Exhaust People

Video-call fatigue is not a character flaw and it is not solved by telling people to focus. It has four identified causes, and every one of them has a mechanical fix in the meeting settings. This is rare among wellbeing topics — the advice is technical rather than exhortative.

Four causes, four fixes
  • Excessive close-up eye contact → use speaker view rather than gallery for groups over eight, and let people sit back from the camera.
  • The load of watching yourself constantly → hide self-view. This one setting does more than any other.
  • Reduced mobility → forty-five minute cap with a hard stop; audio-only segments for discussion-heavy blocks so people can move.
  • Constantly performing attentiveness → the chair asks questions by name, so members are not signalling engagement to the whole grid at once.
The six-line norms card

  • Self-view hidden.
  • Speaker view for groups above eight.
  • Forty-five minutes, hard stop.
  • Camera-optional windows named in the agenda, not granted ad hoc.
  • Audio-only for discussion blocks.
  • Chair calls on people by name.
Do this
  1. Change your own settings first — self-view off, speaker view on.
  2. Put the end time in the meeting title.
  3. Mark the camera-optional segment on the agenda before sending it.

Where it shows up: Any virtual chapter or SEC meeting. Particularly the affiliate-wide calls where people are attending from home after school.

Breadth Over Depth-of-One

The only large study of what CTSO participation does found that the number of different kinds of things a member did — competition, leadership, service, professional development — was what tracked with better outcomes. A member doing one thing brilliantly is the risk profile, not the member doing several things loosely.

Four quadrants, three by May
  • Competition — entered an event, any event.
  • Leadership — held a role, ran a committee, chaired one meeting.
  • Community service — one project, with a real community partner.
  • Professional development — one informational interview, one shadowing visit, one career session.
What the study found, honestly
  • More types of participation went with greater academic motivation and engagement, better grades, higher career self-efficacy, and stronger college aspirations.
  • Of the four elements, competition showed the most positive effects.
  • And: the CTSO students in the study started the year ahead of the comparison group and gained less across it. This Hub does not cite the study as proof that HOSA causes achievement. It supports a breadth recommendation and nothing more.
Why it matters for the chapter

A chapter that only competes loses the members who did not place. A chapter with all four quadrants gives every member a place to be good at something.

Do this

  2. Pick the emptiest one. Do one thing in it before the next meeting.

Conflict Triage

Lead a TeamTeamBUILD15 min

Conflict on a team comes in three kinds, and the wrong response to one kind makes it worse. Before responding to any officer-team conflict, the chair answers one question — which kind is this — and that answer routes to a different script. The single most useful move a student leader can learn is converting a relationship complaint into a task or process question.

The three kinds
  • Task — what are we doing? A disagreement about the goal, the event, the decision. Some of this is healthy.
  • Process — how are we doing it? Who decides, who does what, by when. Usually a charter gap.
  • Relationship — how do we feel about each other. Consistently harmful, and it is never resolved in a meeting.
Three scripts
  • Task → structured debate. Put both positions on the board, give each side two minutes uninterrupted, vote or decide by the rule in the charter.
  • Process → charter amendment. The conflict is a missing rule. Write the rule; the conflict usually dissolves.
  • Relationship → a private conversation between the two people, or with the chair as a third. Not the group chat, not the meeting. Ever.
The conversion

Most relationship complaints have a task or process complaint underneath. ‘She never listens’ is often ‘she made a decision the charter says we make together’. Find the underneath and treat that.

Do this
  1. Next conflict: before responding, write down which of the three it is.
  2. Apply the matching script and nothing else.
  3. If it was relationship, ask what the task or process complaint underneath might be.

Where it shows up: Officer team meetings. SEC. Any committee. It replaces the difficult-conversations models the old build carried.

Coach note
Advisors get pulled into relationship conflict and try to mediate it in front of the team. Do not. Take it private, and ask each person separately what the other one did — the task or process answer is usually right there.
Grounded in

Jehn (1995), Administrative Science Quarterly; De Dreu & Weingart (2003), Journal of Applied Psychology — relationship conflict consistently harmful, task conflict less benign than earlier claimed. Interest-based framing after Fisher & Ury (P).

Fill the Room

Lead a TeamTeamBUILD30 min

Recruitment is a funnel with five stages, and at every stage people leave for a reason. The reason is almost never the message. It is a cost — a meeting time that collides with a bus route, a dues figure nobody explained, a first meeting where everyone else already knew each other. Find the cost at each stage and the funnel fills itself.

Five stages, each with a conversion action and a cost
  • Hears about it → attends once. Action: a named person invites them. Cost: they do not know anyone who will be there.
  • Attends once → joins. Action: they are given something to do before they leave. Cost: dues, and nobody said what they buy.
  • Joins → engages. Action: a real task with a deadline by meeting three. Cost: the meeting time, the location, the transport.
  • Engages → leads. Action: the four-rung handoff of one function. Cost: fear of being the only one who cares.
  • Leads → recruits. Action: they invite the next one, by name.
The cost column is the point

For every stage, write the cost in time, money, social risk and what the member gives up to be there. Most chapters have never written that column. When they do, the fix is nearly always logistics, not messaging.

The first thirty days

Onboarding is the second and third stage done deliberately. One named action per week — a greeting by name at meeting one; a real task by meeting three; a question about what they want from HOSA before week four, with a promise about how the answer will be used.

Do this
  1. Fill the five-stage funnel for your chapter on the printed sheet. Cost column first.
  2. Pick the stage where most people leave. Change one cost.
  3. Write the first-thirty-days sequence and assign one officer to run it for every new member.

Where it shows up: August to October. Again in January for the second-semester intake.

Coach note
When a chapter says nobody comes, ask what time the meeting is and how the members get home. That is usually the answer, and it has nothing to do with the flyer.

Give Feedback That Lands

Lead a TeamTeamEMPOWER10 min

Feedback is the thing a chapter officer does most often and is taught least. One three-part structure — what happened, what the person did, what effect it had — keeps it specific enough to act on and free of the judgement that makes people stop listening.

Situation, behaviour, impact
  • Situation — when and where. ‘At Tuesday’s meeting, during the fundraiser discussion.’
  • Behaviour — what they did, observably. ‘You cut in three times while Sam was talking.’ Not ‘you were rude’.
  • Impact — what it did. ‘Sam stopped contributing and we lost the one person who had run a fundraiser.’
Then stop

Do not add advice unless asked. The three parts are the feedback; the next step is theirs to propose. If they ask, offer one thing, not five.

It works in both directions

The same three parts deliver praise that is worth something. ‘At the health fair, you stayed with the grandmother who was frightened by her results, and she left with a plan. That is the whole reason we were there.’

Do this
  1. Write the next piece of feedback you need to give in the three parts before you give it.
  2. Delete every adjective about the person. Keep every verb about the behaviour.
  3. Give one piece of positive feedback this week in the same three parts.

Where it shows up: After every event. In every one-to-one. It is the everyday tool that Conflict Triage and Hold a Peer Accountable escalate from.

Coach note
Officers give feedback as a character judgement because it is faster. Ask them to say the behaviour line first, before anything else. If they cannot name a behaviour, there is no feedback yet — only a feeling.

Hand Off a Task

Lead a TeamTeamEMPOWER15 min

Delegation fails in one of two ways: the task is thrown over the wall and dropped, or it is handed over and then taken back at the first wobble. There is a four-rung sequence that avoids both, and health science students already know it — it is how a clinical skill is taught.

Four rungs

Same task, four passes, the officer stepping back one rung each time.

  • You do it while they watch. Narrate what you are deciding, not just what you are doing.
  • You do it together. They drive, you are beside them.
  • They do it while you watch. You say nothing unless asked.
  • They do it and report back. You are not in the room.
What each rung is for
  • Rung one makes the invisible decisions visible.
  • Rung two catches the misunderstanding before it becomes a habit.
  • Rung three is the hard one for the officer — the silence is the teaching.
  • Rung four is the only rung that counts as delegation. The first three are training.
Not this

The old build carried a matrix that matched a leadership ‘style’ to a member’s readiness. That model is widely taught and has weak evidence for the prescribed matching. Retired. The four rungs are simpler, and they work.

Do this
  1. Pick one task you keep doing yourself. Name the member who should own it by December.
  2. Schedule the four rungs on four dates.
  3. On rung three, say nothing. Time yourself.

Where it shows up: Every officer who does too much. The advisor side of it is the Student-Led Transfer Ladder — which chapter functions the advisor still performs that a student could.

Coach note
The rung most officers skip is three. They go from doing it together to handing it over, and the member fails, and the officer concludes delegation does not work. Ask which rung they are on. It is nearly always two.

The Committee Report

Lead a TeamVoiceBUILD10 min

Most committee reports tell the meeting what the committee did. The meeting does not need to know that. It needs to know what the committee recommends, so it can decide. Open with the recommendation; the history is available if anyone asks.

The structure

Two minutes, in this order.

  • The recommendation. ‘The fundraising committee recommends a bake sale on 14 February, run by the sophomores, target three hundred dollars.’
  • The one reason. ‘The fun run needs a permit we cannot get in time.’
  • The decision needed. ‘We need a vote to adopt, and a volunteer to book the cafeteria.’
  • Then stop. Questions will surface anything else.
Report versus recommendation

A report informs — here is what happened. A recommendation asks the body to act. The chair needs to know which one is coming, because only the second one gets a motion. Say which it is in the first five words.

If you must tell a story

One shape, when the meeting needs to understand a situation before it can decide: the situation as it was, the complication that changed it, the question that raises, and your answer. Four sentences. It is the only narrative structure this Hub teaches, because one is enough.

Do this
  1. Rewrite your next report with the recommendation as the first sentence.
  2. Cut everything that happened before the recommendation was reached.
  3. Rung 2 of the Speaking Ladder. Record it; check the first sentence.

Where it shows up: February of One Motion a Month, where committee reports are the month’s procedure. Every SEC meeting.

Coach note

Write So People Act

Lead a TeamVoiceBUILD15 min

A chapter email that nobody acts on was not read badly; it was written so that no action was findable. Plain language is a federal standard with a short rulebook, and applying it to a chapter announcement takes five minutes and roughly halves the length.

The rules that do the most work
  • The action first. What do you want them to do, by when. First line, not last.
  • One topic per message. Two topics is two messages.
  • Short sentences, common words, active voice. ‘Bring your form on Tuesday’, not ‘forms should be submitted by attendees prior to the Tuesday session’.
  • Headings and lists for anything longer than a paragraph. Nobody reads a wall.
  • The reader’s question, answered — ‘what does this mean for me’ — before your reason for writing.
For health material, score it

Anything that is patient education — a flyer, a handout, a poster — gets the PEMAT score from Community as well. Plain language is the writing; PEMAT is the check.

For the affiliate

State staff and the SEC can adopt the federal plain-language guidance as a house standard in one meeting. A Chartered Association that asks chapters to communicate clearly should be able to show it does.

Do this
  1. Take the last chapter message you sent. Put the action in the first line.
  2. Cut it by half.
  3. Send the shorter one next time and count the replies.

The Ward Lens

Health outcomes in DC differ sharply from one ward to the next. Knowing that is not the skill. That is the critical level of health literacy, and it is the level a state officer should be modelling.

Start with the published data, not with a story

DC Health publishes ward-level health data. Use the current release, cite it, and date it. Do not carry a figure from memory or from this card — the numbers move and this card deliberately holds none.

  • Pick one outcome. Life expectancy, a chronic condition, access to a provider, a screening rate.
  • Find it by ward in the current DC Health data. Write down the source and the year.
  • Find the ward where it is worst and the ward where it is best.
Then ask what produces the gap

Determinants, not behaviour. The question is never ‘what are people in that ward doing wrong’.

  • Where are the providers, the pharmacies, the grocery stores, the clinics — and how long does it take to reach them without a car?
  • What does housing, income, and school quality look like, and how long has it looked that way?
  • Which past decisions — zoning, transit, hospital closures, where a clinic was built — shaped the current map?
  • Which of those is a decision someone could still make differently?

Bylaws and Standing Rules

Lead BeyondGovernanceBUILD20 min

Two documents govern DC HOSA, and they change by different processes. Knowing which one a rule lives in tells you how hard it is to change and who can do it. Officers who do not know this try to fix a bylaw problem with a motion, or wait a year to fix a standing-rule problem that could have been fixed at the next meeting.

The difference
  • Bylaws — the constitution of the affiliate. Who is a member, what the officers are, how they are elected, how the bylaws themselves change. Amending them requires prior notice and a supermajority, and the process is written into the bylaws.
  • Standing rules — the operating details. Deadlines, forms, the calendar, recognition programme details. Can be adopted or amended by a majority at a meeting where they are in order, without prior notice unless the standing rules say otherwise.
  • Where a standing rule contradicts a bylaw, the bylaw wins. Where practice contradicts a standing rule, the standing rule is what needs amending — practice is not a rule.
The DC situation

DC HOSA runs several things differently from the international model and from its own Standing Rules as currently written — competition delivery, the ILC internal deadline, several calendar dates, the Standards of Excellence deadline. Those divergences are settled decisions within the State Advisor’s authority, and they are on the list to be written into the Standing Rules. Until they are, an officer reading only the Standing Rules will be wrong about the calendar.

  • Read the current Calendar of Events alongside the Standing Rules, not instead of them.
  • If a rule and the calendar disagree, ask the State Advisor which is current. Do not pick.
  • When you find one, log it — that list is how the amendment gets written.
Do this
  1. Read the DC HOSA Bylaws and Standing Rules once, start to finish. It takes under an hour.
  2. List every place where what the affiliate actually does differs from what the Standing Rules say.
  3. Bring the list to the State Advisor. That is a contribution, not a complaint.

Where it shows up: State Executive Council orientation. Any time an officer proposes changing ‘how we do things’.

Coach note
An SEC that has read the governing documents asks better questions all year. Assign the reading in the first month and check it was done — it is the highest-return hour in the officer calendar.

Double-Loop Learning

Lead BeyondGovernanceBUILD15 min

A problem can be fixed twice. Once by fixing the instance — a chapter missed a deadline, so extend it. And once by asking why this kind of problem keeps appearing and changing the thing that produces it. State-level roles are the only ones that see the pattern across every chapter, and that view is wasted if every recurrence gets the first kind of fix.

Single loop

Detect the error, fix it, carry on under the same assumptions. Fast, often necessary, and it guarantees the same problem in a different form next term.

  • A chapter misses the registration deadline → the deadline is extended for them. Solved, this once.
Double loop

Detect the error and examine the assumption that produced it. Slower, and it removes the category.

  • A chapter misses the registration deadline → ask why chapters keep missing this particular deadline. The form is confusing, or the date collides with a predictable school event. Fix the form or move the date. Solved, for everyone, permanently.
The trigger

The third time the same type of issue arrives from a different chapter, stop fixing instances. That is the signal. Log every issue resolved; the log is what makes the third time visible.

Do this
  1. Start the pattern log — date, chapter, issue type, instance count. One line per resolved issue.
  2. When any issue type reaches three, write one sentence stating the assumption behind the system that produced it.
  3. Change the system once. Then watch the log for whether the category stops.

Where it shows up: State staff and SEC work across chapters. Any role that hears the same complaint from more than one place.

Coach note
Most people in a support role measure themselves by how fast they resolve things. This card asks them to measure how many things they made impossible to recur. Those are different numbers and the second is the one that matters.

Precedence — Why Motions Rank

Lead BeyondGovernanceBUILD30 minCE: Parliamentary Procedure

When two motions are on the floor at once, one of them has to be handled first. The ranking is not arbitrary — each motion outranks the one below it because it asks a more urgent question. Read the ladder as a set of reasons and it becomes memorable; read it as a list and it never will be.

The ladder, top to bottom

Higher outranks lower. A motion can be made while a lower one is pending; not while a higher one is.

  • Privileged motions — about the assembly itself, not the business: fix the time to adjourn, adjourn, recess, question of privilege, call for the orders of the day.
  • Subsidiary motions — about the main motion: lay on the table, previous question, limit or extend debate, postpone to a certain time, refer to committee, amend, postpone indefinitely.
  • The main motion — the actual proposal. Lowest, because everything else exists to handle it.
Why the order is what it is
  • Adjourn outranks everything because a body that has to leave cannot keep debating.
  • Previous Question outranks Amend because ‘stop talking and vote’ is a more urgent question than ‘change a word’.
  • Postpone Indefinitely is the lowest subsidiary because it is the least urgent thing you can do to a motion — decline to decide it.
  • Incidental motions — point of order, appeal, division, parliamentary inquiry — have no rank. They arise from whatever is happening and are decided immediately.
Reading a real situation

A main motion is pending. Someone moves to amend. Someone else moves the previous question. Amend is pending; previous question outranks it, so it is in order and is voted first. If it passes, debate ends on the amendment and the chapter votes the amendment, then the main motion.

Do this
  1. Draw the ladder from memory, then check it against the card.
  2. At the next meeting where an amendment is pending, deliberately move the previous question and walk the room through what happens next.
  3. Keep a running list of motions made out of order and what the chair should have said.

Where it shows up: January of One Motion a Month, and the Parliamentary Procedure team event, where the full ladder is tested.

Coach note
Nobody learns precedence from the table. They learn it from the first time a motion is ruled out of order and the chair can say why. Manufacture that moment on purpose.

Five questions, scored 1–4
  • A real team — is membership stable and bounded? Does everyone know who is on it?
  • A compelling direction — can every officer say in one sentence what the year is for?
  • An enabling structure — are roles clear, are meetings designed, is the work divided so it can actually be done?
  • A supportive context — does the chapter have the room, the budget, the advisor time, the school’s backing?
  • Expert coaching — is someone helping the team get better at working together, not only at the task?
Low score, different fix
  • Low direction → a goal-setting problem. Write the one sentence.
  • Low structure → a roles-and-meetings problem. Charter and agenda.
  • Low context → an advocacy problem. The Internal Advocacy Pack goes to an administrator.
  • Low coaching → an advisor problem, or a state-staff one.
  • Low ‘real team’ → the roster is the problem. Who is actually on this?
What was retired

Forming, storming, norming, performing is universally taught and has never held up as a sequence teams pass through. Keep the four words as vocabulary if they help. Do not diagnose a chapter with them.

Do this
  1. Score the five in September at an officer meeting. Do not average.
  2. Take the lowest and apply its fix.
  3. Score again in January. Whether the number moved is the finding.

Where it shows up: Chapter officer teams and the SEC, twice a year. The affiliate-level version is the health record state staff keep per chapter.

Coach note
Chapters diagnose themselves with the wrong word. ‘We have no motivation’ is nearly always a 1 on structure. Run the five questions before accepting any chapter’s own diagnosis.

Force Field

Lead BeyondTeamADVANCE25 min

Every change a team tries to make has forces pushing it forward and forces holding it back. The instinct is to push harder. The finding is that pushing harder often increases the resistance too. The sustainable path is usually to reduce a restraining force — simplify the form, move the date — rather than to shout louder for compliance.

Map both sides

One page. The change you want across the top. Driving forces on the left, restraining on the right, each rated weak, medium, or strong.

  • Example — every chapter submitting officer applications on time. Driving: SEC visibility, peer chapters submitting early, the benefit of more planning time, advisor buy-in. Restraining: competing school deadlines, a confusing form, advisor turnover, no consequence for lateness historically.
Then choose
  • Which restraining force is easiest to reduce? Usually process friction — the form, the timing, the instructions.
  • Which driving force is most worth strengthening? Usually visibility of early adopters.
  • Do one of each. Not everything.
Where it fits

Run it before the January reset on the change the team most needs to make in the second half. Run it before any statewide initiative, as the first page of the launch plan.

Do this
  1. Name the change. Fill both columns on the printed sheet.
  2. Rate each force.
  3. Pick one restraining force to reduce and one driving force to strengthen. Assign each.

Where it shows up: Before any change the team is about to push for. It pairs with Early Adopters First at Governance · Lead Beyond.

Coach note
Teams fill the driving column enthusiastically and leave the restraining column half empty. Make them fill the right side first. The right side is where the plan is.

Hold a Peer Accountable

Lead BeyondTeamEMPOWER10 min

Raising a missed commitment with a peer is the hardest conversation on an officer team, because nobody outranks anybody. The only thing that makes it possible is agreeing the exact words in advance and writing them into the charter. Decided before it is needed, it is a procedure. Decided in the moment, it is a fight.

The script, written in September

The charter states it. Something like:

  • ‘You said you would have X by Y. It is not here. Where does it stand, and what do you need?’
  • The three parts: the commitment as it was made, the fact that it was not kept, an open question. No adjectives. No history.
  • The reply is also scripted: a status, and a new date or a request for help. Not an explanation of why.
Why it works

Because it was agreed. The person raising it is doing what the team decided, not making a personal accusation. The person receiving it heard the words in September and signed them.

The second time

The charter also says what happens the second time. Usually: the task moves to someone else, stated in the meeting, without discussion. That is not punishment. It is what the team agreed.

Do this
  1. Write the exact script into the charter at the first officer meeting. Read it aloud.
  2. The first time it is needed, use the words as written. Do not improve them.
  3. Debrief afterwards, privately, with the person — how did the script land.

Where it shows up: Officer teams and the SEC, from the second month onward. It is the last field of Write the Charter.

Coach note
When an officer tells you a teammate is not pulling their weight, ask whether the script has been used. It almost never has. Do not run the conversation for them; ask them to run it as written and report back.

The January Reset

Lead BeyondTeamBUILD60 min

Teams do not improve gradually. They change sharply at the temporal midpoint of their lifespan, when the deadline suddenly becomes real. An officer term runs September to May; the midpoint is January. A reset scheduled there is not generic advice — it is the one moment the research says a team is most willing to change how it works.

What the midpoint does

Around halfway, a team realises how much time is left and how much of the plan has not happened. That realisation is uncomfortable and it is useful — it is the only point in the year when a team will voluntarily abandon its September assumptions.

The reset, in one meeting
  • Re-score the Five Conditions. Compare to September.
  • Re-read the charter. Amend what has not held.
  • Each officer writes a midpoint memo: what I said I would do, what has happened, what I am changing for the second half. One page.
  • Re-derive the calendar from the second peak — the offsite Round Two dates and SLC.
  • One decision the team has been avoiding, decided.
For the SEC

This is the defensible reason for a mid-year retreat. Not a reward, not a bonding exercise — the point in the year when the council is most able to change course.

Do this
  1. Put the reset in the calendar now, in the first two weeks of January, before anything else claims the date.
  2. Send the midpoint memo template two weeks before.
  3. Run the five parts in order. The avoided decision goes last so it cannot be skipped.

Where it shows up: January, every year, at every level. It is also the second reading of the charter and the second run of the Five Conditions.

Coach note
The reset fails when it is run as a celebration. Open with the September scores and the calendar as it actually stands. Let the gap be visible before anyone talks about what went well.

Make an Ask

Lead BeyondVoiceCONNECT30 minCE: Researched Persuasive Writing & Speaking · Extemporaneous Writing – Health Policy

Youth advocacy fails in a specific way: plenty of voice, no audience. Members write, post, present — and nobody with the power to act was in the room. A four-box checklist tells you whether a campaign is real before you run it, and a 16-year-old can audit their own in ten minutes.

Four boxes — all four, or it is not participation
  • Space — is there a safe, inclusive place for the members to form and express a view?
  • Voice — are they supported to say it? Information, time, the words.
  • Audience — is someone with the power to act actually listening? Named. In the room, or receiving the document.
  • Influence — will the view be acted on, and will the members be told what happened and why?
The box DC students miss

Audience. A campaign with Space and Voice and no Audience is an assembly. DC has something most affiliates lack: a city council whose committees accept public testimony, including from young people. That is an Audience with a calendar.

Then the One-Page Ask

See Testify. The ask is what you put in front of the Audience once you have found one.

Do this
  1. Audit your current campaign against the four boxes. Write yes or no on each.
  2. If Audience is no, stop. Find one — the ward map has candidates; the Council calendar has hearings.
  3. If Influence is no, decide how members will hear back. That is part of the design, not an afterthought.

Where it shows up: Any advocacy the chapter or SEC runs. Researched Persuasive Writing & Speaking and Extemporaneous Writing – Health Policy are this, in competition form.

Coach note
When an officer proposes an awareness campaign, ask who the Audience is by name. If the answer is ‘the community’ or ‘students’, there is no Audience yet, and the campaign is not ready.

Speak for the Affiliate

The moment a state officer is introduced to a hospital partner or a Council staffer, they need to know where their authority ends. Right now they learn it by getting it wrong. This card is written as permissions rather than prohibitions, so it enables rather than chills.

You may, on your own authority
  • Describe DC HOSA, its mission, its pillars, and its programmes accurately.
  • Share published figures — dated, from the membership report — and say when you do not know one.
  • Accept a speaking invitation for yourself, and say you will confirm the affiliate’s participation.
  • Take a partner’s interest back to the SEC and the State Advisor, with a date by which they will hear.
  • Give your own view, labelled as your own.
You may not, without the State Advisor
  • Agree a partnership, sponsorship, or any arrangement with money or the DC HOSA name in it.
  • Quote a figure you have not seen published — membership, chapters, budget.
  • Respond to media on behalf of the affiliate.
  • Sign anything.
  • Commit a chapter to anything. Chapters commit themselves.
The sentence that handles every edge case

‘That is a great question and it is not mine to answer — I will take it to the State Advisor and you will hear back by [date].’ Said with confidence, it reads as competence, not as weakness.

Do this
  1. Read both lists at SEC orientation. Every officer.
  2. Write the edge-case sentence on the back of your name badge, literally.
  3. The first time you use it, tell the State Advisor the same day.

Coach note
This gap is real and the affiliate has been closing it case by case. Write the two lists into SEC orientation and the Portfolio Charter so nobody learns them the hard way. And when an officer uses the edge-case sentence correctly, tell them — that is the behaviour you want repeated.

Testify

Lead BeyondVoiceCONNECT45 minCE: Researched Persuasive Writing & Speaking · Extemporaneous Writing – Health Policy

The DC Council holds public hearings, and its committees accept testimony from members of the public — young people included. Public witnesses get a few minutes each. That is a real Audience with real power, on a published calendar, and almost no chapter has ever used it. The One-Page Ask is what you bring.

The One-Page Ask

Six parts, in order, one page.

  • The problem, in two sentences.
  • The DC-specific data point — a ward disparity from named DC Health data, a pathway gap, a funding line. Cited and dated.
  • The single ask, stated as a verb a named person can perform. Not ‘support health education’. ‘Fund the health science pathway at two more high schools in the FY28 budget.’
  • Who that person is — the committee, the councilmember, the agency.
  • The date by which it must happen, and why that date.
  • What happens next if the answer is no.
At the hearing
  • Check the hearing notice for the witness time limit and the sign-up method. Follow both exactly.
  • Read the One-Page Ask aloud. It fits. Submit the written version for the record.
  • Say who you represent, and what you are and are not authorised to commit. See Speak for the Affiliate.
  • Say ‘I am excited’ in the corridor first.
Afterwards

Report back to the chapter and the SEC — what was said, what the committee asked, what happens next. That closes the Influence box.

Do this
  1. Draft the six parts. Have someone score the ask line: could a named person do this on Monday?
  2. Find the next relevant hearing on the Council calendar. Sign up as a public witness.
  3. Rehearse it as Rung 4 of the Speaking Ladder — timed, scored on the one criterion: the ask is in the first thirty seconds.

Where it shows up: Council hearings on health, education, or the budget. The same document, shortened, is the Internal Advocacy Pack an advisor takes to a principal.

Coach note
The first testimony is the hard one. Go with them. Sit behind them in the hearing room, say nothing, and debrief within 24 hours using the three questions. The second one they do alone.
